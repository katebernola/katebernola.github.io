Phase 1: Identify what doesn't add value

Do the math. Stop the work.

What it looks like

We stopped making Help Center videos

The multimedia team spent more than half its time making videos that mirrored the Help Center articles they lived in. Nobody had done the math.

Get the details → Hide the details

When I inherited the multimedia team, it was "the video team." The designers spent more than half their time making videos that mirrored Help Center articles. No one had ever done the math to analyze the value.

The math

Ready for a trip back to math class?

16 hours to create

×$120 per resource-hour

$1920 per video

$12 savings per contact avoided

×10% chance one view prevents a contact

$1.20 max value per view

$1920 per video

÷$1.20 max value per view

1600 views to break even

Current state

At the old pace of 3 videos a sprint, the cost added up fast.

Help Center videos 237
Videos with 1600+ annual views 12
Videos below ROI threshold 95%
Cost per video $1920
Videos per year 78
Annual savings $150k

What we changed

  • We stopped taking requests.
  • Once each quarter, we looked at the most-read articles without videos.
  • We only made a video if it would add value and the numbers justified it.

Results

Before, the team created 3 videos per sprint. After, they produced 4 videos over the next few years. One was a C-suite ask, but the other three had data to support them.

  • We stopped designing for edge cases

    Designers were spending hours on rare errors. We got Product agreement to ship a generic message first and invest more only when the data showed it was needed.

  • We stopped writing "how to" articles

    POs thought every feature needed multiple Help Center articles. Break-even math and usability findings showed they rarely paid off, so we cut production and upkeep by 90%.

  • We stopped making demos for other teams

    One 30-minute conversation let us return demo videos to the teams that originally produced them. We only needed to create a reusable template.

Key insight

If a conversation doesn't solve it, show the math.

Phase 2: Find surefire wins

Let your people shine. Fuel demand.

What it looks like

We shifted UX involvement left

Two roadmap initiatives, kickoff days away, and nobody knew where to start.

Get the details → Hide the details

The Product team in Tax had hit a wall. They had two roadmap initiatives, both aimed at cutting support calls: one on tax registration, one on filing failures. Kickoff was days away and nobody knew where to start.

I had offered support from designers and researchers several times, but Product worried including them before kickoff would cause churn. So I instructed my team to do the work anyway, and share their discoveries with the PO leading each project.

Reframing the work

My team had already recognized that both initiatives shared the same core problem, and needed one solution. We built a Claude agent to read call logs, record how each issue was resolved, and rank the resolutions by how often we used them.

For each problem, the team

  • Worked with Engineering and Product. How do we know this problem is coming? What signals do we have to warn us?
  • Analyzed the cheapest resolution. Can an existing workflow solve this? Can the chatbot?
  • Mapped alerts. Where does this show up today? More importantly, when do admins need that info?

Defining the channels

Once we could see the patterns, I defined a purpose for each channel. I already owned our communications governance, so that part was quick.

I got Product to agree to add a new deliverable for the project: consistent communication. Separately, I reached out to the platform team, to get these definitions rolled out platform-wide.

Results

Based on overall complexity, our pre-SDLC estimator had put this work at 6 weeks. We completed the effort and finalized product scope within 8 days.

The best part? We didn't have to wait for the next planning cycle to see results. Hours after we shared the work, the POs' manager pushed both kickoffs to give us time to work together.

  • I brought multimedia in earlier

    When we built a new accountant portal, the PO requested a few images.

    I saw an opening to bring multimedia into the design process earlier, so I embedded a multimedia designer on the project.

    He shaped the portal's visual hierarchy and introduced micro-animations that improved usability.

  • I traded a last-minute rescue for early engagement

    A 401(k) rebalancing flow had failed three rounds of usability testing.

    They needed a content design resource, fast, so I asked for a favor in return. They agreed to also bring the designer into the kickoff for an upcoming project, where I knew the content needs would be high.

    He succeeded at both, and the retirement team made him their full-time content designer.

  • I protected a designer from a no-win assignment

    A payroll team asked for support on a workflow so unintuitive, they couldn't even explain it to us.

    I dug in and showed that the real issue was system architecture. No quick fix would solve that; the core concept itself didn't make sense.

    That kept us from setting a precedent that we'd hand out band-aids. It also kept my team from failing at an assignment that was impossible.

Key insight

Build credibility by solving important problems, not solving every problem.

Phase 3: Make great work repeatable

Build tools and frameworks. Multiply impact.

What it looks like

We turned an AI agent into a design coach

Scaling content expertise when headcount was shrinking.

  • We always had something ready to ship

    A leadership change brought a flood of constantly shifting priorities.

    For one video request, I worked with the PO to define the MVP ask. Full video? GIF? Static image? Screenshot of the UI?

    The answer was yes to all. We started with a screenshot, planning to move up the list as time allowed.

    And priorities did change. The promotion shipped with a screenshot.

  • We built an illustration library into our design system

    Product designers were digging through years of old illustrations, leading to inconsistent color and composition.

    We put elements, colors, and layouts at designers' fingertips, by adding our new library into Figma components.

    Designers spent less time on images, while quality went up.

Key insight

Don't scale people; scale expertise.

4

Phase 4: Create the new normal

Persist and be patient. Then get out of the way.

Culture change doesn't happen because a leader says so. It takes permission, credibility, and consistency. Every audience needs a different plan: the team, peers, leaders, and partners.

Prepare your team to advocate for themselves. Looking across the organization, one pattern stood out: the more a designer pushed for a seat at the table, the further the culture shifted.

What it looks like

  • We restructured the team around new expectations

    We moved content designers from a centralized team into the product areas they supported. The reporting model reinforced two expectations: collaboration started at kickoff, and content designers are designers, not writers.

  • I got Product to engage us earlier

    I shifted my team from waiting for Product to define the work, to using data to identify problems and propose strategic solutions. That gave Product concrete, evidence-backed ideas to take into roadmap planning and changed how partners saw UX's role.

Key insight

Designers had to want it enough to be annoying. I had to make that safe.
