Phase 1: Identify what doesn't add value

Do the math. Stop the work.

Case study

Stopping creation of Help Center videos

When I inherited the multimedia team, it was "the video team." The designers spent more than half their time making videos that mirrored Help Center articles. No one had ever done the math to analyze the value.

The math

Ready for a trip back to math class?

16 hours to create

×$120 per resource-hour

$1920 per video

$12 savings per contact avoided

×10% chance one view prevents a contact

$1.20 max value per view

$1920 per video

÷$1.20 max value per view

1600 views to break even

Current state

Help Center videos 237
Videos with 1600+ annual views 12
Videos below ROI threshold 95%

At the old pace of 3 videos a sprint, the cost added up fast.

Cost per video $1920
Videos per year 78
Annual savings $150k

What we changed

  • We stopped taking requests.
  • Once each quarter, we looked at the most-read articles without videos.
  • We only made a video if it would add value and the numbers justified it.

Results

Before, the team created 3 videos per sprint. After, they produced 4 videos over the next few years. One was a C-suite ask, but the other three had data to support them.

Other ways it played out

  • Product design: We stopped designing for rare error cases. The PO shipped a generic message first and only invested more if the data showed it was needed.
  • Content design: Help Center articles made up the bulk of the Content Design team's work. I applied the same break-even math we used for Help Center videos. We cut production and upkeep by 90%.
  • Multimedia design: One 30-minute conversation paved the way to return demo videos to the original producers. All we had to do was create a reusable template.

Key insight

If a conversation doesn't solve it, show the math.

Phase 2: Find surefire wins

Let your people shine. Fuel demand.

Case study

Shifting UX involvement left

The Product team in Tax had hit a wall. They had two roadmap initiatives, both aimed at cutting support calls: one on tax registration, one on filing failures. Kickoff was days away and nobody knew where to start.

I had offered support from designers and researchers several times, but Product worried including them before kickoff would cause churn. So I instructed my team to do the work anyway, and share their discoveries with the PO leading each project.

Reframing the work

My team had already recognized that both initiatives shared the same core problem, and needed one solution. We built a Claude agent to read call logs, record how each issue was resolved, and rank the resolutions by how often we used them.

For each problem, the team

  • Worked with Engineering and Product. How do we know this problem is coming? What signals do we have to warn us?
  • Analyzed the cheapest resolution. Can an existing workflow solve this? Can the chatbot? Or does it need a new build?
  • Mapped alerts. Where does this show up today: the weekly summary, the admin dashboard, before payroll? More importantly, when do admins actually need to know?

Defining the channels

Once we could see the patterns, I defined a purpose for each channel. I already owned our communications governance, so that part was quick.

Map for tax communications

Where it shows up Prominence Goal Use for Status
Pop-up after payroll runs Warn them where they can't miss it Service-blocking issues that start before their next login Build it
Top actions on admin dashboard Put their most important task first, with the next few beside it Actions needed to prevent an error or blocked service Expand to all use cases
System notifications Varies Tell them a system event happened, in the channel they choose: email, push, text, or in-app System events that require communication No change
Weekly summary Remind them regularly, outside our system Any open tax task Send as email only, not a system notification

Other dashboard tiles Show a minor message to someone already using that tile Nothing in tax today —

Consistent communication became a main project deliverable. That closed gaps we'd found along the way. Even better, the channel definitions are now being built into a platform-level schema.

Results

Based on overall complexity, our pre-SDLC estimator had put this work at 6 weeks. We completed the effort and finalized product scope within 8 days.

The best part? We didn't have to wait for the next planning cycle to see results. Hours after we shared the work, the POs' manager pushed both kickoffs to give us time to work together.

What I do

Seek out projects where the need is so obvious, sending in an expert feels like cheating.

Ways it played out

  • Multimedia design: When we completely overhauled our accountant portal, the content designer requested a few images. I embedded a multimedia designer on the project, enabling him to define visual hierarchy for the new portal. He also suggested micro-animations that enhanced usability.
  • Content design: A 401(k) rebalancing flow had failed three rounds of usability testing. Before I committed a resource, I made a bargain: yes, we could do this, but I also wanted early engagement on an upcoming project. The designer completed both with flying colors, and the retirement team made him their full-time content designer.
  • Content design: A payroll team asked for "content support" on a workflow nobody could explain. I proved the real issue was system design. Instead of implementing a band-aid, we focused our time on other projects where we could make an impact.

Key insight

Credibility comes from solving important problems, not from taking every request.

Phase 3: Make great work repeatable

Build tools and frameworks. Multiply impact.

Case study

Scaling expertise with AI

Our company acquired a new platform that nearly doubled our product footprint. At about the same time, content designers began moving into new UX leadership roles, so the team was shrinking.

We couldn't hire more designers, so we decided to make their expertise available to everyone.

The first agent: UI Writing Assistant

Like everyone else, our first instinct was to try AI. We pasted our standards into an AI writing agent and hoped it would coach people through writing better UI copy.

The results were disappointing.

When you provided a rough draft, the AI spat out text that was "translated" into our product voice. But that ignored the real problems: placement, componentry, and the propensity to jam multiple messages together. Plus, the agent couldn't explain why a change was important, so its output was sometimes ignored.

Building the system

Years of guidance existed across wiki pages, design system documentation, and tribal knowledge.

Where guidance lived before: nine different places, plus people's heads.

So we stopped improving the AI, and started improving the knowledge architecture.

  • Audited existing knowledge
  • Consolidated duplicate guidance
  • Added missing guidance
  • Standardized page structure
  • Documented why for everything
  • Assigned ownership
  • Connected related concepts
  • Filled knowledge gaps

The content designers no longer reported to me. Each was embedded with a different product team, and everyone already had a full-time job. Even so, the practice still operated as one team.

I organized the work, divided ownership, and established a shared structure. The team drove the work. They reviewed each other's standards, worked through problems, and held everyone accountable to the timeline.

Over three months, we transformed hundreds of scattered guidelines into one coherent knowledge system.

Growing the system

Once the structure existed, adding knowledge became straightforward.

Instead of documenting rules, we documented decisions.

The second agent: content design coach

By the time we'd completed documentation, we had access to more advanced AI tooling. But this time, we taught it our strategy. We also told it to push back, suggest alternate approaches, and call out risks. The new agent wasn't a UI writer. It was a content designer.

How the AI content design coach works, end to end.

Results

More than 100 people across UX and partner organizations adopted the AI coach.

Content designers could invest expertise where it mattered. They built new strategy, improved information architecture, and shaped the integration of LLMs into our products.

Other ways it played out

  • Product design: Before creating a new solution, we began with a simple plan with elements that already existed. As we upgraded pieces, we always had something ready-to-ship. This enabled us to pivot on a dime, while also leaving dev with something to code.
  • Multimedia design: A reusable illustration library let designers assemble graphics from standardized pieces instead of creating every illustration from scratch.

Key insight

Don't scale people; scale expertise.

4

Phase 4: Create the new normal

Persist and be patient. Then get out of the way.

Making it stick

Culture change doesn't happen because a leader says so. It takes permission, credibility, and consistency. Every audience needs a different plan: the team, peers, leaders, and partners.

Prepare your team to advocate for themselves. Looking across the organization, one pattern stood out: the more a designer pushed for a seat at the table, the further the culture shifted.

Ways it played out

  • Content designers went from ticket-takers to embedded partners on every product team, connected by a community of practice.
  • Product designers shifted from spending weeks in execution to providing data-based arguments that influenced the roadmap.
  • Multimedia design shifted from asset-creation to collaborative partnership, enabling the team to shape when and how multimedia was used.

Key insight

Leaders can create the conditions for change, but the team has to own it.

Raising craft

The framework made room for great work. It didn't teach anyone how to do it.

With experienced designers, my job was to raise the bar: spot gaps in the work, introduce the right methods, ask the questions we were missing, and use evidence to challenge the obvious solution. Sometimes I coached from the sidelines. Sometimes I got directly involved.

Nine of my twelve content designers were new to the discipline, so they needed something different. I hired for aptitude and attitude, then taught them the craft. It wasn't long before their skills surpassed my own.

Case study

Replacing loud promotion with quiet visibility

Our app's biggest navigational update in years started with a content request for an in-app promotion.

The request came in with minimal information.

Overview
Add a walkthrough for new users. As the last step, point them to Perks.
Business goal
Increase new user awareness of Perks
What is the value to the company, in dollars?
We make $20 off of every user who visits Perks. But many users don't know it exists, especially new users. We need to make sure that every new user is aware of Perks.

Here's what they were asking for.

Here's the thing ... we'd had a walkthrough before, so we knew exactly how this would go. About two-thirds of users decided "not for me" and never started. Of those who started, about two-thirds dropped off after the first step. Only 8% saw the final step.

8% is promotional reach, not engagement. We didn't know how many of that 8% would click a Perks promotion.

We'd tried plenty of ways to promote Perks

Perks had been a business priority since it launched. But there was no target for what "enough" adoption looked like. Any lift was worth keeping, and the business wanted more.

Make it a Task

In production

Pros

  • Persistent until users visited Perks
  • Non-blocking

Cons

  • Poor visibility. Tasks was below the fold, and the Dashboard showed only the number of open tasks.
  • Unknown impact. The Task and menu item had always been live; together, the existing experience produced an 18% first-month visit rate.
  • Devalued the pattern. Tasks were intended for things users actually needed to do.
  • Risked trust. Users complained about being tricked into opening an ad disguised as a Task.
Add a banner to Tasks

In production · later discontinued

Pros

  • Persistent
  • Non-blocking
  • Moderate impact. New-user visits increased from 18% to 23%.

Cons

  • Poor visibility. It still lived behind a below-the-fold Tasks tile.
  • Added noise to the Dashboard.
Add a "New" badge

Used at Perks launch

Pros

  • High impact. Users who saw the treatment were 35% more likely to visit than the control.
  • Persistent until action
  • Non-blocking
  • Prompted action by creating a strong drive to clear the notification indicator.

Cons

  • Different audience. The result came from an existing-user launch; we didn't know whether it would work the same way when the entire product was new.
  • Perks wasn't new anymore.
  • Devalued the pattern. Promotional use weakened the meaning of "New" when we actually needed to communicate new functionality.
  • Risked trust. A false "New" signal teaches users that product cues may really be ads.
Show a pop-up at login

Previously used

Pros

  • Maximum visibility
  • High immediate impact. 24% of users who saw it visited Perks.

Cons

  • Blocking. It interrupted users before they could do what they'd logged in to do.
  • One-time. It produced an immediate bump, not sustained visibility.
  • Lower-quality traffic. Visits increased, but interaction with Perks decreased.
  • Risked trust. Promotional interruptions made it harder to know when an interruption was actually important.

We don't need to hijack a pattern to get attention

A Task should still mean something users need to do. "New" should still mean new. An interruption should still signal something worth interrupting them for.

We could make Perks more prominent without asking another pattern to mean something it didn't.

We needed a different approach

I looked back at the promotions we'd run, and defined requirements for a real solution.

  • Visible
  • Persistent
  • Non-blocking
  • Aligned to user expectations

The prior promotions either demanded attention or waited for users to stumble across them. I wanted Perks to have a consistent, persistently visible place in the app's navigation, without interrupting what users came to do.

What if Perks had a permanent place in the app's navigation?

The experiment

Changing mobile navigation is no small feat. We're talking standing up a new initiative; getting funding and roadmap priority; assigning a PO, dev team, and other resources; then starting pre-SDLC.

The business certainly didn't have that kind of patience to pursue an idea proposed by UX. But I had an even better idea about how we could test this for free.

Pendo was already layered over the product, so I asked our Pendo builder a slightly weird question: could we put four linked images across the bottom of every screen and make them look and behave like a rudimentary bottom nav?

His response:

"Uhhh, maybe??"

🤔

"Theoretically, yes. Let me see if I can build it."

He could.

So I pulled in just enough expertise to make it a responsible experiment:

  • Pressure-tested the idea with our senior product strategist. We made sure this wasn't a clever idea we'd all come to regret, then chose the four destinations together. About 20 minutes.
  • Brought in our principal visual designer. He made the faux navigation look native to the product. Less than a day.
  • Defined the audience and success metrics. I validated both with a UX researcher and systems data analyst over chat.
  • Crossed the Ts, dotted the Is, and pushed it live.

We tested it live

Pendo randomly split eligible new users 50/50 between a control group and a test group. We measured activity on both groups simultaneously, for 30 days after first login.

The control group had the existing experience: Perks was available through the menu and as a Task. The test group got that, plus the faux bottom nav on every screen.

Our primary KPI was whether users visited Perks.

Persistent visibility drove a 3.4× lift

The results were compelling.

18% of users visited Perks when they saw the normal experience.

61% visited when we added the bottom nav.

Our simple A/B test showed that we didn't need interruptions, badges, and promotional UI to drive attention. By making Perks persistently visible in a place that made sense in the app's IA, we could get a much larger lift.

The band-aid requests stopped

The test changed the conversation. Instead of finding another way to promote Perks, we could solve the underlying problem with persistent mobile navigation.

The business turned their attention to making that a reality, and stopped asking for one-off promotional treatments. And they succeeded. An initiative to create a mobile bottom nav was approved for the roadmap, and is on its way to development.
